Macbeth quotes quiz (GCSE English Literature)

Twelve questions on the quotes that come up most: who says them, when, and what they show. Then the key quotes, themes and context to go back over.

For GCSE English Literature: AQA, Edexcel, OCR and Eduqas · Updated November 2026

Macbeth in a nutshell

  1. The story: a brave Scottish soldier hears a prophecy that he'll be king, murders King Duncan, and is destroyed by guilt and his own ambition.
  2. Big themes: ambition, power and kingship, guilt, the supernatural, and appearance versus reality.
  3. Context: written around 1606 for King James I, soon after the Gunpowder Plot.

The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Act 1

1. Who says "Fair is foul, and foul is fair"?

The witches chant it in the very first scene. It sets up the theme of appearance versus reality: in this play, things are not what they seem. Macbeth echoes it when he first appears ("So foul and fair a day I have not seen").

Act 1

2. "Look like th' innocent flower, / But be the serpent under't." Who says this, and to whom?

Lady Macbeth tells Macbeth to hide his murderous plans behind a welcoming face. The serpent links her to the devil in the Garden of Eden: another example of appearance versus reality.

Act 1

3. "Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires." What does this show about Macbeth?

Macbeth says this in Act 1 Scene 4, after Malcolm is named heir. He is already thinking about killing to become king. The contrast of light and dark runs through the play.

Act 1

4. In the "Vaulting ambition" speech, what does Macbeth admit?

He has "no spur" except "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself". He knows he has no good reason to kill Duncan, and that ambition can make you fall, like a rider jumping too far onto a horse.

Act 2

5. When does Macbeth say "Is this a dagger which I see before me"?

In Act 2 Scene 1, just before the murder, Macbeth sees a floating dagger. It could be a hallucination caused by guilt, or something supernatural, and it shows how troubled his mind already is.

Act 2

6. "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" What does this show?

Straight after killing Duncan, Macbeth feels that even the whole ocean couldn't wash away his guilt. Blood becomes a symbol of guilt throughout the play.

Act 2

7. Who says "A little water clears us of this deed"?

Lady Macbeth dismisses the murder as something easily washed away, the opposite of Macbeth's reaction. By Act 5, she can't stop trying to wash imaginary blood from her hands.

Act 3

8. "O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!" What does this suggest?

Becoming king hasn't brought peace. Macbeth is tortured by fear and suspicion, especially of Banquo and his son Fleance, whom the witches said would be kings.

Act 3

9. True or false: Banquo's ghost appears to Macbeth at the banquet.

True. In Act 3 Scene 4, only Macbeth can see the ghost. His wild reaction in front of his guests shows his guilt, and starts to reveal what he has done.

Act 5

10. What is happening when Lady Macbeth says "Out, damned spot!"?

In Act 5 Scene 1, a doctor and a gentlewoman watch her sleepwalk. The woman who said "A little water clears us of this deed" is now destroyed by guilt.

Act 5

11. Macbeth's "Life's but a walking shadow" speech comes just after he hears that…

In Act 5 Scene 5, Macbeth hears of his wife's death and sees life as empty and meaningless. It shows how far his ambition has left him with nothing.

Act 5

12. Who calls Macbeth and Lady Macbeth "this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen"?

Malcolm, the new king, says it in the final scene. Compare it with the start, when Macbeth was "brave Macbeth": the play has turned a hero into a butcher.

Key quotes to learn

  • "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" (the witches, 1.1): appearance versus reality, the supernatural.
  • "brave Macbeth—well he deserves that name" (the Captain, 1.2): Macbeth starts as a loyal hero.
  • "Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires" (Macbeth, 1.4): hidden ambition.
  • "unsex me here" and "Look like th' innocent flower, / But be the serpent under't" (Lady Macbeth, 1.5): she rejects womanhood, and deception.
  • "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself" (Macbeth, 1.7): ambition as his only motive.
  • "Is this a dagger which I see before me" (Macbeth, 2.1): a troubled mind, the supernatural.
  • "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" (Macbeth, 2.2) and "A little water clears us of this deed" (Lady Macbeth, 2.2): guilt.
  • "O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!" (Macbeth, 3.2): paranoia.
  • "Out, damned spot!" (Lady Macbeth, 5.1): guilt destroys her.
  • "Life's but a walking shadow" (Macbeth, 5.5): despair.
  • "this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen" (Malcolm, final scene): how far they've fallen.

Themes: key points

  • Ambition: it drives Macbeth to murder and destroys him. Lady Macbeth's ambition is just as strong at first.
  • Power and kingship: Duncan and Edward of England are good kings; Macbeth is a tyrant. Malcolm restores order at the end.
  • Guilt: shown through blood, sleeplessness and hallucinations. It affects both Macbeths, at different times.
  • The supernatural: the witches, the dagger and Banquo's ghost. Do the witches cause events, or just tempt Macbeth?
  • Appearance versus reality: people hide their true intentions; the witches' prophecies are true but misleading.
  • Masculinity: Lady Macbeth challenges Macbeth's manhood to push him into the murder.

Context: key points

  • Written around 1606, early in the reign of King James I, who was also King of Scotland and claimed descent from Banquo.
  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605) was a failed attempt to kill James, so killing a king (regicide) was a shocking topical subject.
  • People believed in the divine right of kings: the king was chosen by God, so killing him went against God and nature.
  • James was fascinated by witchcraft and wrote a book about it, Daemonologie.
  • Jacobean audiences expected women to be obedient to their husbands, which makes Lady Macbeth's behaviour more shocking.

Revising Macbeth

The exam is closed book, so learning short quotes is the key. A few things that tend to help:

  • Learn short quotes of a few words. They're easier to remember and easier to fit into a sentence.
  • Learn each quote with a theme and a character, so you can use it in more than one essay.
  • Quiz yourself little and often: say the start of the quote and finish it, or say who says it and when.
  • Link to context where it helps your argument, not as a separate paragraph.

Common questions

Is the GCSE English Literature exam open book?

No. Since 2017, GCSE English Literature exams are closed book for all the main exam boards, so you need to learn quotes from memory.

How many Macbeth quotes should I learn?

Around 10 to 20 short quotes, covering the main characters and themes, is a good target. Short quotes you know well are more useful than long ones you half remember.

What does the Macbeth exam question look like?

For most boards you're given an extract from the play and asked about a character or theme, both in the extract and in the play as a whole. Check the exact format with your teacher.

When was Macbeth written?

Around 1606, during the reign of King James I. It's Shakespeare's shortest tragedy.

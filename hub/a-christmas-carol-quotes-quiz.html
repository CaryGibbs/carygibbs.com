A Christmas Carol quotes quiz (GCSE English Literature)

Twelve questions on the quotes and moments that come up most, stave by stave. Then the key quotes, themes and context to go back over.

For GCSE English Literature: AQA, Edexcel, OCR and Eduqas · Updated November 2026

A Christmas Carol in a nutshell

  1. The story: on Christmas Eve, the miser Ebenezer Scrooge is visited by Marley's ghost and three spirits, and is transformed into a generous man.
  2. Big themes: redemption, social responsibility, poverty, family, and the spirit of Christmas.
  3. Context: written by Charles Dickens in 1843, to make readers care about the poor in Victorian Britain.

The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Stave 1

1. Scrooge is described as "solitary as an oyster". What does the simile suggest?

Like an oyster, Scrooge is closed off and alone, with a hard outer shell. There may be a hint of hope too: an oyster can hide a pearl, and Scrooge does change.

Stave 1

2. Who does Scrooge ask "Are there no prisons?"

When two gentlemen ask for money for the poor, Scrooge says the prisons and workhouses are enough. This reflects harsh Victorian attitudes to poverty, which Dickens wanted to challenge.

Stave 1

3. What does Scrooge mean when he says the poor should die and "decrease the surplus population"?

Scrooge echoes the ideas of Thomas Malthus, who argued the population was growing too fast. In Stave 3, the Ghost of Christmas Present throws the words back at him when he worries about Tiny Tim.

Stave 1

4. Marley says "I wear the chain I forged in life". What does the chain stand for?

Marley's chain is made of cash-boxes, keys, padlocks and ledgers: the greed of his life. He warns that Scrooge's chain is even longer.

Stave 1

5. Who says "Mankind was my business"?

When Scrooge says Marley was a good man of business, Marley's ghost replies that people, not money, should have been his business. It's Dickens's main message.

Stave 2

6. Which kind, generous employer does the Ghost of Christmas Past show Scrooge?

Fezziwig, Scrooge's old boss, throws a joyful Christmas party for his workers. He shows how much an employer can affect people's happiness, a contrast with how Scrooge treats Bob Cratchit.

Stave 2

7. Belle ends her engagement to Scrooge, saying "Another idol has displaced me". What has replaced her?

Belle says a "golden" idol has taken her place in Scrooge's heart. His love of money has cost him love and family.

Stave 3

8. What are the two children hidden under the Ghost of Christmas Present's robe called?

The boy is Ignorance and the girl is Want. The Ghost warns to beware them both, but most of all the boy. Dickens is warning society about the dangers of ignoring poverty and lack of education.

Stave 4

9. True or false: the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge a future in which Tiny Tim has died.

True. The Cratchits grieve for Tiny Tim. Scrooge is also shown people stealing from and celebrating the death of an unloved man, who turns out to be Scrooge himself.

Stave 4

10. Which ghost never speaks?

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come is silent, hooded and only points. Its silence makes it the most frightening of the spirits, and leaves Scrooge to face the future himself.

Stave 4

11. When does Scrooge promise "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year"?

Scrooge says it at his own grave, begging the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come for a chance to change. In Stave 5, he wakes up and keeps his promise.

Stave 5

12. What is Dickens's main purpose in writing A Christmas Carol?

Dickens wanted to change attitudes. Scrooge's transformation shows readers that anyone can change, and that we all have a responsibility to help others. By the end he becomes "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man" as the city knew.

Key quotes to learn

  • "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!" (narrator, Stave 1): Scrooge's greed.
  • "solitary as an oyster" (narrator, Stave 1): isolation.
  • "Are there no prisons?" and "decrease the surplus population" (Scrooge, Stave 1): attitudes to the poor.
  • "I wear the chain I forged in life" and "Mankind was my business" (Marley's ghost, Stave 1): consequences and responsibility.
  • "Another idol has displaced me" (Belle, Stave 2): money over love.
  • "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want." (Ghost of Christmas Present, Stave 3): Dickens's warning to society.
  • "God bless Us, Every One!" (Tiny Tim, Stave 3): hope and goodness despite poverty.
  • "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year" (Scrooge, Stave 4): his promise to change.
  • "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew" (narrator, Stave 5): redemption.

Themes: key points

  • Redemption: Scrooge shows that anyone can change, however late.
  • Social responsibility: Dickens argues that the rich have a duty to help the poor.
  • Poverty: the Cratchits are poor but loving; Ignorance and Want show the dangers of ignoring poverty.
  • Family: the Cratchits and Fred's party contrast with Scrooge's lonely life.
  • Christmas: a time of generosity, kindness and togetherness.
  • Time: past, present and future show how choices shape a life.

Context: key points

  • Published in 1843, in Victorian London, at a time of huge gaps between rich and poor.
  • The Poor Law Amendment Act (1834) sent many poor people to harsh workhouses.
  • Dickens knew poverty: as a child he worked in a blacking factory while his father was in a debtors' prison.
  • Some people followed Thomas Malthus, who argued that the population was growing faster than food supplies.
  • Dickens's story helped shape the modern idea of Christmas as a time for family and generosity.

Revising A Christmas Carol

The exam is closed book, so learning short quotes is the key. A few things that tend to help:

  • Learn one or two quotes per stave, so you can track how Scrooge changes.
  • Link each quote to a theme and to Dickens's purpose.
  • Quiz yourself little and often: say the start of the quote and finish it, or say who says it and when.
  • Remember it's a novella with a message: ask what Dickens wants the reader to think or do.

Common questions

Is the GCSE English Literature exam open book?

No. Since 2017, GCSE English Literature exams are closed book for all the main exam boards, so you need to learn quotes from memory.

Why is A Christmas Carol divided into staves?

A stave is a verse of a song. Dickens called his chapters staves to match the idea of a Christmas carol.

How many quotes should I learn for A Christmas Carol?

Around 10 to 15 short quotes, with at least one from each stave and some for each main theme, is a good target.

What does the exam question look like?

For most boards you're given an extract and asked about a character or theme, in the extract and in the novella as a whole. Check the exact format with your teacher.

Year 9 GCSE options: a parent's guide

Choosing GCSE options can feel like a big decision for a 13 or 14 year old. Here's what we've found helps, from one parent to another.

By Cary Gibbs, dad of two and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About a 6-minute read

A note before you start. I'm a dad, not a careers adviser. Options were one of those moments where we weren't sure how much to steer and how much to step back. This is what we've learned, but every school runs options differently, so their options booklet and evening come first. It follows the system in England. In Wales options are also usually chosen in Year 9; in Northern Ireland GCSE choices are usually made in Year 10; and in Scotland, students usually choose their National 4 and 5 courses in S3.

The short version

  1. Everyone takes the core: usually English, maths and science. Options are the extra subjects on top.
  2. Every school does it differently, so the options booklet and options evening are the most important things to read and go to.
  3. Interest and ability matter most. Students tend to do best in subjects they enjoy and are good at.
  4. Keep doors open if they don't know what they want to do later. A broad mix rarely closes anything off.
  5. It's their choice, with your help. Ask questions rather than deciding for them.

1. How options work

Most schools ask students to choose their GCSE options in Year 9, usually in the spring term, ready to start in Year 10 (some start a year earlier). Typically:

  • Core subjects everyone takes: English language, English literature, maths and science (either combined science, worth two GCSEs, or the three separate sciences). Many schools add religious studies or a language.
  • Option subjects they choose, often three or four, from "blocks" set by the school's timetable.
  • Most students end up with around 9 or 10 GCSEs in total, though this varies.

Schools usually run an options evening and publish a booklet describing each subject, how it's assessed and what it leads to. Those are the most useful things you'll get.

2. What's changing

This section is about England. For years, many schools steered students towards the EBacc: English, maths, sciences, a language, and history or geography. The government has decided to scrap the EBacc as a school performance measure, following its curriculum and assessment review, and has said it wants a broader mix, including arts subjects, and an entitlement to triple science. Changes take time to reach schools, so your child's school may still be adjusting what it asks for. The options booklet will say what applies this year.

3. Helping them choose

What's helped us is asking good questions and letting them do the thinking:

  • What do you enjoy? Two years is a long time to study something you dislike.
  • What are you good at? Look at reports and test results together, and ask teachers.
  • How is it assessed? Some subjects are all exams, others include coursework (NEA). Some students prefer one over the other.
  • What might you want to do after 16? If they have an idea, check what sixth forms, colleges or courses ask for. If not, that's completely normal.
  • Are you choosing it for the right reasons? Following friends or a favourite teacher is understandable, but friends change classes and teachers move on.

4. Questions worth asking at options evening

  • How is this subject assessed, and how much coursework is there?
  • What do students need to be doing now to do well in it?
  • Is it offered at foundation and higher tier?
  • Can my child take triple science, and how is that decided?
  • What happens if a subject is oversubscribed, or doesn't run?
  • Can students change an option early in Year 10 if it isn't working?

5. If they're not sure

Most 13 and 14 year olds don't know what they want to do, and that's fine. A balanced mix keeps the most doors open: for example, a humanities subject, a creative or practical subject, and something they simply enjoy. The school's careers adviser and form tutor can help too.

Common questions

When do students choose their GCSE options?

Usually in the spring term of Year 9, though some schools do it in Year 8. The school will tell you the dates and hold an options evening.

How many GCSEs should my child take?

Most students take around 9 or 10, including the core subjects. The school decides the structure; more isn't always better.

Is the EBacc still required?

The government has said it will scrap the EBacc as a school performance measure, and schools are adjusting. Check your school's options booklet for what it asks for this year.

What if my child picks the wrong option?

Talk to the school early. Some allow changes at the start of Year 10, and a single option rarely decides a future path.

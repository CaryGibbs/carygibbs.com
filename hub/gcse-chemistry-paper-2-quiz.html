The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Rates of reaction

1. Why does increasing the temperature make a reaction faster?

Hotter particles move faster, so they collide more often, and a bigger share of collisions have at least the activation energy. The activation energy itself doesn't change (that's what a catalyst does).

Rates of reaction

2. What does a catalyst do?

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing a different pathway with a lower activation energy. It isn't used up, and it doesn't change how much product you get.

Equilibrium

3. True or false: at equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions happen at exactly the same rate.

True. Equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the forward and reverse reactions go at the same rate, so the amounts of reactants and products stop changing. Both reactions are still happening.

Organic chemistry

4. What is the general formula for the alkanes?

Alkanes are CnH2n+2: for example methane CH4, ethane C2H6, propane C3H8 and butane C4H10. CnH2n is the alkenes.

Organic chemistry

5. Compared with long-chain hydrocarbons, short-chain hydrocarbons are…

The shorter the chain, the lower the boiling point, the runnier (less viscous) it is and the more easily it burns. That's why short chains such as petrol are in high demand as fuels.

Organic chemistry

6. What do you see when bromine water is shaken with an alkene?

Alkenes decolourise bromine water: it goes from orange to colourless. With an alkane, it stays orange. This is the test for alkenes, which are made by cracking.

Chemical analysis

7. On a chromatogram, a spot moves 3.0 cm and the solvent moves 7.5 cm. What is the Rf value?

Rf = distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent = 3.0 ÷ 7.5 = 0.4. An Rf value is always less than 1.

Chemical analysis

8. Which gas relights a glowing splint?

Oxygen relights a glowing splint. Hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop, carbon dioxide turns limewater milky (cloudy), and chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper white.

The atmosphere

9. Roughly what makes up today's atmosphere?

About 80% nitrogen and 20% oxygen, with small amounts of other gases such as carbon dioxide, water vapour and noble gases. The early atmosphere was mostly carbon dioxide.

The atmosphere

10. Which gas in the air causes acid rain?

Sulfur dioxide (and oxides of nitrogen) cause acid rain. Carbon monoxide is a toxic gas, methane is a greenhouse gas, and nitrogen makes up most of the air without causing harm.

Using resources

11. In the UK, fresh water is made safe to drink (potable) by…

UK water comes from rain, so it's filtered and then sterilised with chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light. Distillation and reverse osmosis are used to desalinate sea water, which takes far more energy.

Using resources

12. True or false: potable water is pure water.

False. Potable water is safe to drink, but it isn't pure in the chemical sense: it still has small amounts of dissolved substances. It just has low levels of salts and microbes.

Rates and equilibrium: key points

  • Rate of reaction = amount of reactant used, or product made, ÷ time.
  • Collision theory: particles only react if they collide with at least the activation energy.
  • Rate goes up with higher concentration (or pressure for gases), larger surface area (smaller pieces) and higher temperature.
  • Catalysts give a different pathway with a lower activation energy and aren't used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts.
  • Reversible reactions go both ways (⇌). If one direction is exothermic, the other is endothermic, transferring the same amount of energy.
  • Equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the forward and reverse reactions go at the same rate.
  • Higher tier: Le Chatelier's principle. If conditions change, the equilibrium shifts to counteract the change.

Organic chemistry: key points

  • Crude oil is a finite resource, mostly made of hydrocarbons (compounds of hydrogen and carbon only).
  • Alkanes are CnH2n+2. Learn the first four: methane, ethane, propane, butane.
  • Fractional distillation separates crude oil into fractions with similar boiling points.
  • Shorter chains: lower boiling point, less viscous, more flammable.
  • Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide and water.
  • Cracking (catalytic or steam) breaks long chains into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes.
  • Alkenes turn bromine water from orange to colourless.
  • Separate Chemistry only: alkene reactions, alcohols, carboxylic acids, and addition and condensation polymers.

Chemical analysis: key points

  • A pure substance is a single element or compound, with a sharp melting and boiling point.
  • A formulation is a mixture designed as a useful product, such as paint, fuel or medicine.
  • Chromatography separates mixtures. Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent.
  • Gas tests: hydrogen, squeaky pop; oxygen, relights a glowing splint; carbon dioxide, limewater turns milky; chlorine, bleaches damp litmus paper.
  • Separate Chemistry only: flame tests, tests for ions, and instrumental methods.

The atmosphere: key points

  • Today's air is about 80% nitrogen and 20% oxygen, with small amounts of carbon dioxide, water vapour and noble gases.
  • The early atmosphere was mostly carbon dioxide, released by volcanoes. Oxygen built up as algae and plants photosynthesised.
  • Carbon dioxide fell as it dissolved in oceans, was used by plants and algae, and was locked up in sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels.
  • Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour) keep the Earth warm. Human activity is adding more, causing climate change.
  • Pollutants: carbon monoxide is toxic; sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause acid rain; particulates cause global dimming and health problems.

Using resources: key points

  • Potable water is safe to drink but not pure. In the UK it's filtered, then sterilised with chlorine, ozone or UV light.
  • Desalination (distillation or reverse osmosis) makes sea water drinkable but needs lots of energy.
  • Waste water treatment: screening, sedimentation, anaerobic digestion of sludge and aerobic treatment of effluent.
  • A life cycle assessment looks at a product's impact from raw materials to disposal.
  • Reduce, reuse, recycle saves raw materials and energy.
  • Higher tier: phytomining and bioleaching extract metals from low-grade ores.
  • Separate Chemistry only: corrosion, alloys, ceramics and composites, and the Haber process.

Revising for Paper 2

Paper 2 has plenty of facts to learn (gas tests, formulas, the order of the early atmosphere), which makes it well suited to short, regular quizzing. A few things that tend to help:

  • Quiz first, then check the notes. Trying to remember is what makes it stick.
  • Come back to topics over several days. Little and often lasts longer than one long session.
  • Practise the calculations, such as Rf values and rates from graphs, until they're automatic.
  • Check your exam board and tier. This page follows AQA. Higher tier and separate Chemistry add extra content.

Common questions

What topics are on AQA GCSE Chemistry Paper 2?

Topics 6 to 10: the rate and extent of chemical change, organic chemistry, chemical analysis, the chemistry of the atmosphere, and using resources. Paper 2 can also ask about ideas from Paper 1, such as atomic structure and bonding.

Is Combined Science Paper 2 the same as Chemistry Paper 2?

They cover the same five topics, but separate (triple) Chemistry has extra content, such as alcohols, polymers, ion tests and the Haber process. This page notes which parts are separate Chemistry only.

How long is the Chemistry Paper 2 exam?

For AQA, separate Chemistry Paper 2 is 1 hour 45 minutes and Combined Science: Trilogy Chemistry Paper 2 is 1 hour 15 minutes. Check your exam timetable for the exact dates.

I'm with a different exam board. Is this still useful?

Much of it, yes. Edexcel, OCR and WJEC cover similar chemistry, but the topics are grouped differently across papers. Check your board's specification, and use Lobe Limpet to turn your own revision list into quizzes.

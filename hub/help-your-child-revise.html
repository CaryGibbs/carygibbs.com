How to help your child revise for their GCSEs

You don't need to know the subjects. Here's what actually works, and how to help without it turning into nagging.

By Cary Gibbs, parent and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About an 8-minute read

The short version

  1. Start from the revision list, or the exam board's specification, so they know exactly what to cover.
  2. Swap re-reading for self-testing. Recalling an answer makes it stick far better than reading notes again.
  3. Little and often. 20 to 30 focused minutes most days beats a long session at the weekend.
  4. Come back to topics over days and weeks, just as they start to fade.
  5. Practise the real thing: past papers and written answers, not just recognising the right option.
  6. Your job is support, not supervision. Ask them to teach you something, notice the effort, and protect their sleep.

1. Know what they need to revise

The most common revision problem isn't laziness. It's not knowing where to start. Before anything else, find out exactly what's being tested.

  • Ask for the revision list. Most schools hand one out before tests and mocks. If your child hasn't got one, it's fine to email the subject teacher and ask.
  • Find out the exam board for each subject. In England, GCSEs are set by AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR or WJEC Eduqas, and different subjects at the same school often use different boards. Your child's teachers or the school website will say.
  • Look at the specification. Each exam board publishes a free specification for every subject, listing everything that can come up. It's the most reliable checklist there is.

A useful first session together is simply going through the list and sorting topics into three piles: confident, a bit shaky, and no idea. That tells them where to spend their time, and it's a calm, practical conversation rather than a lecture.

2. The revision methods that actually work

Many students revise by re-reading notes, highlighting and copying things out neatly. It feels productive, and the material starts to look familiar, but familiar isn't the same as remembered. A well-known 2013 review of learning techniques by John Dunlosky and colleagues found that two methods stood out as the most useful, and highlighting and re-reading were among the least.

Test yourself (retrieval practice)

Pulling an answer out of memory is what strengthens it. That's why quizzes, flashcards, past paper questions and the "blank page" method (write down everything you can remember about a topic, then check) work so well. Getting things wrong is part of it: a wrong answer, then the right one, is often remembered better than a right answer that was easy.

Space it out (spaced practice)

Revising a topic once, however thoroughly, isn't enough. Coming back to it a day later, then a few days later, then a week or two later is what makes it last until exam day. It's why cramming the night before feels like it works for a test the next morning, and why it's mostly gone a month later.

Mix it up

Switching between topics in a session (a bit of chemistry, some French vocabulary, then a history question) is harder than sticking to one, and that's exactly why it helps. It's closer to a real exam, where questions don't arrive in neat order.

Explain it

Explaining something in their own words, out loud or in writing, shows up the gaps fast. Written answers matter a lot at GCSE and A level, so practising them, not just recognising the right option, is time well spent.

3. How much, and when to start

There's no magic number, but a sensible guide is:

  • Years 7 to 9: 10 to 20 minutes on a school night when a test is coming up. The aim is building the habit.
  • Year 10 and early Year 11: 20 to 40 minutes most days, a little and often, building up before mocks.
  • From mocks to the summer exams: around an hour or two on school days, more in the holidays, in blocks of 25 to 45 minutes with short breaks.

When to start? Earlier than they think, but smaller than you fear. GCSE exams usually run from May into June, and many schools hold Year 11 mocks between November and February. Ten minutes a day from the autumn of Year 11 does more than a panicked Easter holiday, because spaced practice needs time to work.

4. Making a simple plan

A plan stops the daily "what should I do?" decision, which is often where revision stalls. Keep it simple:

  1. Work back from the exam dates. Put them on a calendar where everyone can see them.
  2. Give each day one or two subjects, with the shakiest topics coming up more often.
  3. Build in reviews: a few minutes each day going back over older topics, not just new ones.
  4. Leave rest days. A plan with no slack falls apart at the first busy week.

Let your child make the plan, with you as a sounding board. People stick to plans they made themselves.

5. How you can help (without nagging)

Teenagers revise better with a parent who is interested than with one who is checking up on them. A few things that genuinely help:

  • Ask them to teach you. "Explain photosynthesis to me" is one of the best revision activities there is, and you don't need to know the answer to ask the question.
  • Notice the effort, not just the results. "You've done something every day this week" builds the habit far more than "is that all?".
  • Ask "what's next?" rather than "have you revised?". It gets a real answer instead of a defensive one.
  • Make it easy to start: a tidy place to work, snacks, a set time, and the phone in another room for 25 minutes.
  • Protect their sleep. Memories are consolidated during sleep, so a late-night cram can undo the day's work.
  • Keep perspective. One bad test or a missed day isn't a disaster. What counts is getting back to it the next day.

Things worth avoiding

  • Comparing them with siblings, friends or yourself at their age.
  • Turning every conversation into one about revision.
  • Judging revision by hours at a desk. Twenty focused minutes beats two hours of half-reading.
  • Testing them like an interrogation. If you quiz them, keep it light, and let them check the answers.

6. KS3, GCSE and A level

KS3 (Years 7 to 9)

Tests usually follow whatever the class has just covered, so the school's revision list or the last few weeks of class notes are the place to start. This is the best time to build the habit of testing themselves, with low stakes and short sessions. There's more in our KS3 revision tips.

GCSE (Years 10 and 11)

The specification and past papers become the main guide. Knowing the content isn't enough on its own: exam technique, written answers and timing matter too. Mocks are the dress rehearsal, so the feedback from them is worth more than the grade.

A level (Years 12 and 13)

Less content is taught in class time, so more has to come from independent study. The same methods apply, with more practice at extended written answers. Spacing matters even more, because the first-year topics come back in the final exams.

7. If they're stressed or stuck

Some nerves before exams are normal, and can even help. Signs it's become more than that include trouble sleeping, not eating, avoiding revision altogether, or seeming withdrawn for days at a time.

  • Talk to the school. Form tutors, heads of year and pastoral teams deal with exam stress every year and can adjust support.
  • See your GP if anxiety or low mood carries on or gets worse.
  • Childline is free and confidential for young people: call 0800 1111 or visit childline.org.uk.

If they're stuck on a subject rather than stressed, ask the teacher first. Teachers would much rather know in November than find out in the summer.

Common questions

How long should my child revise each day for GCSEs?

In Year 10 and early Year 11, 20 to 40 focused minutes most days is plenty. From mocks onwards, around one to two hours on school days, in blocks of 25 to 45 minutes with breaks. Consistency matters more than length.

When should my child start revising for their GCSEs?

A little from the start of Year 11 (or earlier) works far better than a lot at Easter. Many schools hold mocks between November and February, which is a natural point to build a routine.

I don't understand the subjects. Can I still help?

Yes. Asking them to explain a topic to you, helping them make a plan, and noticing their effort all help, and none of them need subject knowledge. Quizzes and flashcards come with the answers, so you can check without knowing the content.

Is it better to revise with music on?

For most people, music with lyrics makes it harder to take in words. Quiet, or instrumental music at a low volume, tends to work better. The phone being in another room usually matters more than the music.

My child says they "just read through their notes". Is that enough?

Usually not. Re-reading makes notes feel familiar without making them stick. Suggest turning the notes into questions and testing themselves instead: the same time, much better results.

What should we do after the mocks?

Go through the papers together with the feedback. The topics they dropped marks on become the start of the revision plan for the summer.

The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Cell biology

1. Where in a cell does aerobic respiration happen?

Aerobic respiration happens in the mitochondria. The nucleus holds the genetic material, ribosomes make proteins, and chloroplasts are where photosynthesis happens.

Cell biology

2. Which of these is true of bacterial (prokaryotic) cells?

Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus: their DNA is a single loop, and many also have small rings called plasmids. They are much smaller than plant and animal cells, and do have a cell membrane.

Cell biology

3. What is diffusion?

Diffusion is the spreading out of particles, giving a net movement from an area of higher concentration to lower concentration. It needs no energy from respiration (active transport does).

Cell biology

4. True or false: in osmosis, water moves from a more concentrated solution to a more dilute one.

False. Osmosis is the movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution to a more concentrated one.

Cell biology

5. What does mitosis produce?

Mitosis produces two genetically identical cells, for growth and repair. Meiosis (Paper 2) makes four gametes, each with half the number of chromosomes.

Organisation

6. Why does an enzyme stop working at a high temperature?

High temperatures (and the wrong pH) change the shape of the active site, so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.

Organisation

7. Which blood vessels carry blood away from the heart?

Arteries carry blood away from the heart, under high pressure, so they have thick muscular walls. Veins carry blood back to the heart and have valves; capillaries are where substances are exchanged.

Organisation

8. Which part of the blood helps it to clot?

Platelets are fragments of cells that help blood to clot at a wound. Red blood cells carry oxygen, white blood cells fight infection, and plasma carries everything else.

Infection and response

9. How does a vaccine protect you?

A vaccine contains a dead or inactive form of the pathogen. White blood cells respond by making antibodies, so if the real pathogen gets in later, they can respond quickly before you become ill.

Infection and response

10. True or false: antibiotics can be used to treat flu.

False. Flu is caused by a virus, and antibiotics only kill bacteria. Painkillers can ease the symptoms but don't kill pathogens.

Bioenergetics

11. What is the word equation for photosynthesis?

Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using energy from light. Photosynthesis is endothermic. Aerobic respiration is the reverse: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.

Bioenergetics

12. What does anaerobic respiration in human muscles produce?

In muscles, anaerobic respiration turns glucose into lactic acid. In yeast it makes ethanol and carbon dioxide (fermentation). It releases much less energy than aerobic respiration.

Cell biology: key points

  • Eukaryotic cells (plants and animals) have a nucleus; prokaryotic cells (bacteria) don't, and are much smaller.
  • Animal cells: nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria, ribosomes. Plant cells also have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a permanent vacuole.
  • Magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object. Electron microscopes have much higher magnification and resolution than light microscopes.
  • Cells specialise (differentiate) to do particular jobs, such as sperm, nerve and root hair cells.
  • Mitosis makes two identical cells for growth and repair. Stem cells can become many types of cell.
  • Diffusion and osmosis need no energy. Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.
  • Separate Biology only: culturing microorganisms.

Organisation: key points

  • Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organisms.
  • Digestive enzymes: carbohydrases (such as amylase) break down carbohydrates, proteases break down proteins, lipases break down fats. Bile helps digest fats.
  • Enzymes work by the lock and key model, and are denatured by high temperature or the wrong pH.
  • The heart pumps blood around a double circulatory system. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins carry it back, and capillaries exchange substances.
  • Blood: red blood cells carry oxygen, white blood cells fight infection, platelets help clotting, plasma carries everything else.
  • Coronary heart disease is treated with stents, statins or, in some cases, transplants.
  • Non-communicable diseases have risk factors such as diet, smoking, alcohol and lack of exercise.
  • In plants, xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots, and phloem carries dissolved sugars (translocation). Water is lost through stomata (transpiration).

Infection and response: key points

  • Pathogens are bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists. Examples: measles (virus), salmonella and gonorrhoea (bacteria), rose black spot (fungus), malaria (protist).
  • They spread by air, water and direct contact. Spread is reduced by hygiene, isolating infected people, destroying vectors and vaccination.
  • The body's first defences: skin, hairs and mucus in the nose, cilia in the trachea and bronchi, and stomach acid.
  • White blood cells engulf pathogens (phagocytosis), and make antibodies and antitoxins.
  • Antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses. Overuse leads to resistant bacteria.
  • New drugs are tested for safety and effectiveness in preclinical and clinical trials, often double blind with a placebo.
  • Separate Biology only: monoclonal antibodies and plant diseases.

Bioenergetics: key points

  • Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. It's endothermic, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.
  • The rate is limited by light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature.
  • Plants use glucose for respiration, and to make starch (for storage), cellulose (for cell walls), fats and oils, and proteins.
  • Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. It's exothermic and happens in mitochondria.
  • Anaerobic respiration: glucose → lactic acid in muscles; glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide in yeast.
  • During exercise, heart rate, breathing rate and breath volume increase to supply more oxygen.
  • Higher tier: oxygen debt, and interpreting graphs of limiting factors.

Revising for Paper 1

Paper 1 has a lot of vocabulary and processes to explain. A few things that tend to help:

  • Quiz first, then check the notes. Trying to remember is what makes it stick.
  • Come back to topics over several days. Little and often lasts longer than one long session.
  • Learn the definitions word for word, such as diffusion, osmosis and active transport. Mark schemes look for key words.
  • Practise the required practicals: what was measured, what was changed and what was kept the same.

Common questions

What topics are on AQA GCSE Biology Paper 1?

Topics 1 to 4: cell biology, organisation, infection and response, and bioenergetics.

Is Combined Science Paper 1 the same as Biology Paper 1?

They cover the same main topics, but separate (triple) Biology has extra content. This page notes which parts are separate Biology only.

How long is the Biology Paper 1 exam?

For AQA, separate Biology Paper 1 is 1 hour 45 minutes and Combined Science: Trilogy Biology Paper 1 is 1 hour 15 minutes. Check your exam timetable for the exact dates.

Which required practicals are in Paper 1?

For Combined Science: microscopy, osmosis, food tests, enzymes (the effect of pH on amylase) and photosynthesis. Separate Biology adds microbiology. Questions can ask how you'd carry them out and how to improve them.

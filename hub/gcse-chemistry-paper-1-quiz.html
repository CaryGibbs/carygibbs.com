The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Atomic structure

1. What are isotopes?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so they have different mass numbers. Atoms that have gained or lost electrons are ions.

Atomic structure

2. An atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. What is its mass number?

Mass number = protons + neutrons = 11 + 12 = 23. Electrons have almost no mass, so they aren't counted. The atomic number is 11 (the number of protons), so this is sodium.

The periodic table

3. True or false: the Group 1 alkali metals get more reactive as you go down the group.

True. Going down Group 1, the outer electron is further from the nucleus, so it's lost more easily. Group 7 is the other way round: the halogens get less reactive going down.

The periodic table

4. Why are the Group 0 noble gases so unreactive?

Noble gases have a stable arrangement of electrons, with a full outer shell, so they don't easily gain, lose or share electrons. They exist as single atoms.

Bonding

5. Why does sodium chloride conduct electricity when it's melted or dissolved, but not as a solid?

In a solid ionic lattice, the ions are held in place. When melted or dissolved, the ions are free to move, so they can carry charge.

Structure and properties

6. Why can graphite conduct electricity, when diamond can't?

In graphite each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, so one electron from each atom is delocalised and can move through the structure. In diamond each carbon forms four bonds, so there are no free electrons.

Quantitative chemistry

7. What is the relative formula mass (Mr) of carbon dioxide, CO2? (Ar: C = 12, O = 16)

Mr = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44. Add up the relative atomic masses of every atom in the formula, remembering the small numbers.

Quantitative chemistry

8. True or false: in a closed system, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

True: this is the law of conservation of mass, as no atoms are made or lost. In an open container the mass can seem to change if a gas escapes or is taken in from the air.

Chemical changes

9. What does an acid make when it reacts with a metal carbonate?

Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. With a metal, an acid gives a salt and hydrogen; with an alkali or a metal oxide, a salt and water.

Chemical changes

10. Which of these pH values is acidic?

Acids have a pH below 7, 7 is neutral, and alkalis are above 7. Acids produce hydrogen ions (H+) in water; alkalis produce hydroxide ions (OH−).

Chemical changes

11. Why is aluminium extracted by electrolysis instead of by heating with carbon?

Carbon can only reduce oxides of metals less reactive than itself, such as iron. Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so it's extracted by electrolysis, which uses a lot of energy.

Energy changes

12. In an exothermic reaction, what happens to the temperature of the surroundings?

Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings, so the temperature rises (combustion and neutralisation, for example). Endothermic reactions take energy in, so the temperature falls.

Atomic structure and the periodic table: key points

  • Atoms have a nucleus of protons (charge +1, mass 1) and neutrons (charge 0, mass 1), with electrons (charge −1, very small mass) in shells around it.
  • Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. Atoms have no overall charge, as protons and electrons are equal.
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
  • Electron shells fill in order: up to 2, then 8, then 8 (for the first 20 elements).
  • The model of the atom changed: plum pudding, then the nuclear model after the alpha scattering experiment, then Bohr's electron shells, then Chadwick's neutron.
  • Mendeleev left gaps in his table for elements not yet discovered. Today's table is ordered by atomic number.
  • Group 1 metals react with water to make a hydroxide and hydrogen, and get more reactive going down. Group 7 halogens get less reactive going down. Group 0 noble gases are unreactive.
  • Mixtures are separated by filtration, crystallisation, simple and fractional distillation, and chromatography.

Bonding, structure and properties: key points

  • Ionic bonding (metal + non-metal): electrons are transferred, making oppositely charged ions held in a giant lattice. High melting points; conducts when melted or dissolved.
  • Covalent bonding (non-metals): atoms share pairs of electrons.
  • Simple molecules have low melting and boiling points (weak forces between molecules) and don't conduct.
  • Giant covalent structures such as diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide have very high melting points. Graphite conducts because of its delocalised electrons.
  • Graphene is a single layer of graphite; fullerenes are hollow carbon molecules, including nanotubes.
  • Metallic bonding: positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Metals conduct and can be bent; alloys are harder because different-sized atoms stop the layers sliding.
  • Separate Chemistry only: nanoparticles and their uses.

Quantitative chemistry: key points

  • Conservation of mass: no atoms are made or lost in a reaction, so equations must balance.
  • Relative formula mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses (Ar) of all the atoms in the formula.
  • Concentration (g/dm3) = mass of solute ÷ volume of solution. 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3.
  • Higher tier: moles = mass ÷ Mr, and using moles to balance equations and find limiting reactants.
  • Separate Chemistry only: percentage yield, atom economy, and gas volumes and concentrations in mol/dm3.

Chemical changes: key points

  • Reactivity series: more reactive metals displace less reactive ones from their compounds.
  • Oxidation is gaining oxygen and reduction is losing oxygen. Metals less reactive than carbon are extracted by reducing their oxides with carbon.
  • Acids and salts: acid + metal → salt + hydrogen; acid + alkali or metal oxide → salt + water; acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  • pH scale: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline. Neutralisation: H+ + OH− → H2O.
  • Electrolysis: positive ions go to the negative electrode (cathode) and negative ions to the positive electrode (anode). Used for metals more reactive than carbon, such as aluminium.
  • In aqueous solutions, hydrogen forms at the cathode if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; oxygen forms at the anode unless a halide is present.
  • Higher tier: oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (OIL RIG), half equations, and strong and weak acids.

Energy changes: key points

  • Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings, so the temperature rises: combustion, neutralisation, many oxidation reactions.
  • Endothermic reactions take in energy, so the temperature falls: thermal decomposition, and citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate.
  • Reaction profiles show the energy of reactants and products, and the activation energy: the minimum energy needed for particles to react.
  • Higher tier: calculating energy changes from bond energies. Breaking bonds takes energy in; making bonds gives energy out.
  • Separate Chemistry only: cells, batteries and fuel cells.

Revising for Paper 1

Paper 1 mixes facts to learn (charges, group trends, products of reactions) with calculations and explanations. A few things that tend to help:

  • Quiz first, then check the notes. Trying to remember is what makes it stick.
  • Practise explaining properties from structure, such as why graphite conducts or why ionic compounds have high melting points. These come up often.
  • Do a few calculations every session: Mr, concentration and, for Higher, moles.
  • Check your exam board and tier. This page follows AQA. Higher tier and separate Chemistry add extra content.

Common questions

What topics are on AQA GCSE Chemistry Paper 1?

Topics 1 to 5: atomic structure and the periodic table; bonding, structure and the properties of matter; quantitative chemistry; chemical changes; and energy changes.

Is Combined Science Paper 1 the same as Chemistry Paper 1?

They cover the same five topics, but separate (triple) Chemistry has extra content, such as transition metals, nanoparticles, atom economy and fuel cells. This page notes which parts are separate Chemistry only.

How long is the Chemistry Paper 1 exam?

For AQA, separate Chemistry Paper 1 is 1 hour 45 minutes and Combined Science: Trilogy Chemistry Paper 1 is 1 hour 15 minutes. Check your exam timetable for the exact dates.

Do I need to learn the periodic table?

No. You're given a periodic table in the exam, but you do need to know how to use it: the trends in each group, and how to read atomic numbers and relative atomic masses.

Exam access arrangements: a parent's guide

Extra time, a reader, a separate room: what access arrangements are, how schools decide, and how you can help, explained simply.

By Cary Gibbs, dad of two and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About a 4-minute read

A note before you start. I'm a dad, not a SENCo or a specialist. This is a short, plain-English overview to help you know what to ask. The rules are set by the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ) and applied by your child's school, so for anything about your own child, talk to their SENCo.

The short version

  1. Access arrangements help students with a particular need take their exams fairly, without making the exam easier.
  2. The school decides and applies, following strict JCQ rules. It starts with the SENCo.
  3. It must be their normal way of working, so the support needs to be in place in class and in mocks, not just the summer.
  4. Start early. If you have concerns, raise them in Year 9 or early Year 10.

1. What access arrangements are

Access arrangements are adjustments that let a student with specific needs, such as a learning difficulty, a disability, a medical condition or a temporary injury, show what they know in an exam. They don't change what's being tested. Common examples include:

  • Supervised rest breaks
  • 25% extra time
  • A reader or computer reader
  • A scribe, or speech recognition software
  • Using a word processor (laptop)
  • A separate or smaller room

2. Who decides

The school, usually through its SENCo, identifies what a student needs based on evidence and how they normally work in class and tests. For some arrangements, like 25% extra time, the school has to gather evidence and apply through JCQ's system before a deadline in the exam year. Recent JCQ guidance has also asked schools to try options such as supervised rest breaks before applying for extra time, where that fits the student's needs.

Parents can't apply directly, and a letter from home isn't part of the evidence. But you can help the school see the full picture.

3. How you can help

  • Raise concerns early, ideally in Year 9 or early Year 10, so there's time to assess and build up evidence.
  • Share what you know: any diagnosis, medical letters or previous assessments.
  • Check the support is used in class and mocks. Arrangements need to be the student's normal way of working, and mocks are a good chance to practise with them.
  • Ask what's in place, and when it will be confirmed, well before the summer exams.
  • If something happens close to the exams, such as an injury or illness, tell the school straight away. There are separate arrangements for that.

JCQ publishes a short guidance sheet for parents, carers and students that's worth reading.

Common questions

Can I apply for extra time for my child?

No. The school applies, following JCQ's rules and with evidence of need and normal way of working. Talk to the SENCo if you think your child needs support.

Does my child need a diagnosis?

Not always. It depends on the arrangement and the need. The school's assessor and SENCo will explain what evidence is required.

What if we disagree with the school's decision?

Ask the SENCo to explain the decision and what evidence they looked at. If you're still unhappy, follow the school's complaints process.

What's the difference between access arrangements and special consideration?

Access arrangements are planned in advance for a known need. Special consideration is a small adjustment applied after the exam, if something happened at the time, such as illness or a bereavement.

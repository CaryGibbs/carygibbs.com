The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Homeostasis

1. What is homeostasis?

Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions, such as body temperature, blood glucose and water levels, to keep them at the best levels for cells and enzymes to work.

The nervous system

2. What is the correct order of a reflex arc?

Stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector → response. Reflexes are fast and automatic because they don't involve the conscious part of the brain.

Hormones

3. Which hormone lowers the level of glucose in the blood?

Insulin, made by the pancreas, causes glucose to move from the blood into cells. In liver and muscle cells, extra glucose is stored as glycogen. (Higher tier: glucagon does the opposite.)

Hormones

4. True or false: in Type 2 diabetes, the pancreas makes no insulin.

False. In Type 1 diabetes, the pancreas makes too little insulin. In Type 2, the body's cells no longer respond properly to insulin. Obesity is a risk factor for Type 2.

Inheritance

5. How many chromosomes does a human egg or sperm cell have?

Gametes are made by meiosis, so they have half the usual number: 23 chromosomes instead of 46. At fertilisation the full number is restored.

Inheritance

6. Two parents are both Bb, where b is a recessive allele. What is the chance their child shows the recessive characteristic (bb)?

A Punnett square for Bb × Bb gives BB, Bb, Bb and bb. Only bb shows the recessive characteristic, so the chance is 1 in 4, or 25%.

Inheritance

7. What is DNA?

DNA is a polymer made up of two strands forming a double helix. It's found in chromosomes, and a gene is a small section of DNA that codes for a particular protein.

Inheritance

8. Which pair of sex chromosomes does a typical male have?

Males usually have XY and females XX. Eggs always carry an X, while sperm carry either an X or a Y, so there's a 50% chance of each.

Evolution

9. Which best describes natural selection?

Natural selection, as described by Charles Darwin, means individuals with characteristics best suited to the environment are more likely to survive and breed, passing on their alleles. Choosing which animals to breed is selective breeding.

Ecology

10. In a food chain, what is a producer?

Producers, usually green plants or algae, make glucose by photosynthesis. Food chains start with a producer, followed by primary, secondary and tertiary consumers.

Ecology

11. Which method is used to estimate the number of a plant species in a field?

Placing quadrats at random, counting the plants in each, then scaling up to the area of the field gives an estimate. A transect is used to see how a population changes across an area.

Ecology

12. True or false: deforestation increases biodiversity.

False. Cutting down forests destroys habitats and reduces biodiversity. It also releases carbon dioxide and means fewer trees take it in.

Homeostasis and response: key points

  • Homeostasis keeps internal conditions steady, using receptors, coordination centres (brain, spinal cord, pancreas) and effectors (muscles, glands).
  • The nervous system sends fast electrical impulses along neurones. Reflexes are automatic and don't involve the conscious brain.
  • The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood. Effects are slower but last longer. The pituitary gland is the "master gland".
  • Blood glucose: insulin from the pancreas lowers it; excess glucose is stored as glycogen. Type 1 diabetes: too little insulin. Type 2: cells stop responding to insulin.
  • Reproductive hormones: oestrogen, progesterone, FSH and LH control the menstrual cycle; testosterone is the main male hormone.
  • Contraception can be hormonal (pill, implant, injection) or non-hormonal (condoms, diaphragm, IUD).
  • Higher tier: glucagon, thyroxine, adrenaline, how hormones interact in the menstrual cycle, and hormones to treat infertility (IVF).
  • Separate Biology only: the brain, the eye, body temperature control, the kidneys and plant hormones.

Inheritance, variation and evolution: key points

  • Sexual reproduction joins two gametes and gives variation. Asexual reproduction uses mitosis and makes identical clones.
  • Meiosis makes four gametes, each with half the chromosomes, all genetically different.
  • DNA is a double helix. A gene codes for a protein. The genome is all of an organism's genetic material.
  • Key words: allele, dominant, recessive, homozygous, heterozygous, genotype (the alleles) and phenotype (the characteristic).
  • Use Punnett squares to predict crosses. Polydactyly is caused by a dominant allele; cystic fibrosis by a recessive one.
  • Natural selection (Darwin) explains evolution. Evidence includes fossils and antibiotic-resistant bacteria.
  • Selective breeding and genetic engineering change organisms on purpose, with benefits and concerns.
  • Classification: Linnaeus's system (kingdom down to species), and the three-domain system (archaea, bacteria, eukaryota).
  • Separate Biology only: DNA structure in detail, Mendel, speciation, and cloning.

Ecology: key points

  • Organisms in a community depend on each other (interdependence) for food, shelter, pollination and seed dispersal.
  • Abiotic factors are non-living (light, temperature, water, carbon dioxide). Biotic factors are living (food, predators, pathogens, competition).
  • Adaptations can be structural, behavioural or functional. Extremophiles live in very harsh conditions.
  • Food chains start with a producer. Predator and prey populations rise and fall in cycles.
  • Sampling: quadrats estimate population size; transects show changes across an area.
  • The carbon cycle and water cycle recycle materials through living things and the environment.
  • Human impacts: pollution, land use, deforestation, peat bog destruction and global warming reduce biodiversity. Breeding programmes, protected habitats and hedgerows help protect it.
  • Separate Biology only: decomposition, trophic levels, pyramids of biomass and food security.

Revising for Paper 2

Paper 2 mixes processes to explain (hormones, natural selection) with skills such as genetic crosses. A few things that tend to help:

  • Quiz first, then check the notes. Trying to remember is what makes it stick.
  • Come back to topics over several days. Little and often lasts longer than one long session.
  • Practise Punnett squares until they're quick, and say the probability as a fraction, percentage or ratio.
  • Draw the cycles from memory: the carbon cycle, the reflex arc and the menstrual cycle hormones.

Common questions

What topics are on AQA GCSE Biology Paper 2?

Topics 5 to 7: homeostasis and response; inheritance, variation and evolution; and ecology. Paper 2 can also ask about ideas from Paper 1.

Is Combined Science Paper 2 the same as Biology Paper 2?

They cover the same main topics, but separate (triple) Biology has extra content. This page notes which parts are separate Biology only.

How long is the Biology Paper 2 exam?

For AQA, separate Biology Paper 2 is 1 hour 45 minutes and Combined Science: Trilogy Biology Paper 2 is 1 hour 15 minutes. Check your exam timetable for the exact dates.

Which required practicals are in Paper 2?

For Combined Science: reaction time and field investigations (quadrats and transects). Separate Biology adds plant responses and decay. Questions can ask how you'd carry them out and how to improve them.

School and exam jargon, explained in plain English

We remembered exams from our own school days, but a lot has changed since then: new grades, new terms, and different systems across the UK. This is the list we wish we'd had.

By Cary Gibbs, dad of two and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About a 6-minute read

A note before you start. I'm a dad, not a teacher. A lot has changed since we were at school, and as our daughters moved up through secondary school we kept meeting new terms. Most of this guide follows the system in England, where we live, and there's a section on how it works in Wales, Northern Ireland and Scotland. Your child's school is always the best place to check the details.

The short version

  1. Key stages are the stages of school: KS3 is Years 7 to 9, KS4 (GCSEs) is Years 10 and 11.
  2. GCSEs in England are graded 9 to 1, with 9 the highest. Wales and Northern Ireland use letters (A* to G), and Scotland has its own qualifications.
  3. Tiers (foundation or higher) decide which grades a paper can give in some subjects.
  4. The specification is the official list of what can come up in the exam.
  5. Mocks are practice exams, usually in Year 11 and Year 13.

1. Stages and years

These are the terms used in England. Year names and stages are different in Northern Ireland and Scotland (see Across the UK).

  • Key Stage 3 (KS3): Years 7, 8 and 9, roughly ages 11 to 14. No national exams; schools set their own tests. Our KS3 revision tips cover this stage.
  • Key Stage 4 (KS4): Years 10 and 11, ages 14 to 16, when most students take GCSEs. Some schools start GCSE courses in Year 9.
  • Key Stage 5 (KS5), or sixth form: Years 12 and 13, ages 16 to 18: A levels, T levels, BTECs and other courses, at school or college.
  • Options: the GCSE subjects students choose, usually in Year 9, alongside the core subjects everyone takes. See our Year 9 options guide.
  • Core subjects: the ones every student studies to GCSE, usually English, maths and science.

2. Grades

  • 9 to 1 (England): GCSEs in England are graded 9 (highest) to 1 (lowest), with U for ungraded. A 9 is above the old A*, and a 4 lines up roughly with the old C.
  • Standard pass (grade 4) and strong pass (grade 5): the government's terms. A 4 in English and maths matters most: students without one carry on studying those subjects after 16.
  • Combined science: worth two GCSEs, so it's given a double grade such as 6-5 or 7-7.
  • Working at, predicted and target grades: a working-at grade is where a teacher thinks your child is now; a predicted grade is what they expect by the exam; a target grade is an aim, often set from earlier data. Schools use these differently, so ask what yours means.
  • Grade boundaries: the marks needed for each grade, set after the exams are marked. They change a little each year.

GCSEs in Wales and Northern Ireland are graded with letters instead, and Scotland has different qualifications altogether. See Across the UK.

3. Exams and papers

  • Exam board: the organisation that writes and marks the exams, such as AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR or WJEC Eduqas. Different subjects at the same school often use different boards.
  • Specification (or "spec"): the official document listing everything that can be tested in a subject. It's free on the exam board's website and is the best revision checklist there is.
  • Tiers (foundation and higher): in some subjects, like maths, the sciences and languages, students sit either foundation papers (grades 1 to 5) or higher papers (grades 4 to 9). The school decides, usually in Year 11. Foundation isn't a failure; it's often the right choice for a secure grade.
  • Past papers and mark schemes: real exams from previous years, and the answers examiners were looking for. Free from the exam boards.
  • Mocks: practice exams, usually in Year 11 (and Year 13), run like the real thing. See Mock exams: what parents need to know.
  • NEA (non-exam assessment) or coursework: work done during the course that counts towards the grade, in subjects like art, design and technology, drama and computer science.
  • JCQ: the Joint Council for Qualifications, which sets the rules for exams and publishes the exam timetables.

4. Support words

  • SENCo (or SENDCo): the teacher who leads support for students with special educational needs and disabilities.
  • SEND: special educational needs and disabilities.
  • EHCP: an education, health and care plan, a legal document setting out support for a child with more complex needs.
  • Access arrangements: changes that help a student with a particular need take their exams, such as extra time or a reader. See our guide to access arrangements.
  • Special consideration: a small adjustment to marks if something happens around the time of the exam, such as illness or a bereavement. The school applies for it.
  • Pastoral team: the staff (form tutor, head of year) who look after wellbeing rather than a subject.

5. After 16

  • A levels: two-year courses in Years 12 and 13, graded A* to E. Most students take three.
  • T levels: two-year technical courses equal to three A levels, with an industry placement.
  • BTECs and vocational courses: courses focused on a sector, assessed through a mix of coursework and exams.
  • Apprenticeships: paid jobs with training, from age 16.
  • UCAS: the service students apply to university through, usually in Year 13.

6. Across the UK

Each UK nation runs its own education system. If you've moved, or your child takes exams from boards in different nations, these are the main differences.

England

  • Years 7 to 11 at secondary school, with Key Stage 3 (Years 7 to 9) and Key Stage 4 (Years 10 and 11).
  • GCSEs graded 9 to 1. Exam boards include AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR and WJEC Eduqas.

Wales

  • Years 7 to 11, like England. The Curriculum for Wales describes learning in progression steps rather than key stages.
  • GCSEs are graded with letters, A* (highest) to G. New "Made-for-Wales" GCSEs started in September 2025, with more from September 2026, and they keep the A* to G grades.
  • Most GCSEs in Wales are set by WJEC.

Northern Ireland

  • Secondary school runs from Year 8 to Year 14. Key Stage 3 is Years 8 to 10, and GCSEs are usually taken in Years 11 and 12.
  • Most GCSEs are set by CCEA and graded with letters, A* to G, including a C* grade that sits between B and C. Some schools also use English boards, so a student may get a mix of letters and numbers.

Scotland

  • Secondary years are S1 to S6. S1 to S3 is the "broad general education", and qualifications usually start in S4.
  • Instead of GCSEs and A levels, students take National 4 and National 5 (roughly GCSE level), then Highers (the main university entry qualification) and Advanced Highers. National 5, Higher and Advanced Higher are graded A to D.
  • Exams are set by Qualifications Scotland, which replaced the SQA in February 2026.

As a rough guide to how GCSE grades line up: a 9 in England is about the same as an A* in Northern Ireland, a 7 lines up with an A, and a 4 with a C.

Common questions

What is a good GCSE grade?

It depends on what comes next. A 4 is a standard pass and a 5 a strong pass. Sixth forms and colleges often ask for 4s, 5s or 6s, and sometimes higher in the subjects a student wants to continue. Check the entry requirements of the places your child is interested in.

Is grade 9 the top or the bottom?

The top. GCSEs in England go from 9 (highest) to 1 (lowest). In Wales and Northern Ireland, GCSEs use letters, with A* the highest.

Who decides if my child sits foundation or higher?

The school, usually based on mocks and classwork during Year 11. If you have questions about the choice, ask the subject teacher.

What's the difference between the specification and the syllabus?

They mean the same thing. Exam boards call it the specification.

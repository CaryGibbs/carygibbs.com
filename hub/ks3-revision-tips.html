KS3 revision tips: helping your child revise in Years 7 to 9

When our youngest started secondary school, we weren't sure how much revision KS3 tests needed, or how to help. This is what we've found so far, one parent to another.

By Cary Gibbs, dad of two and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About a 6-minute read

A note before you start. I'm a dad, not a teacher. With our younger daughter in the early years of secondary school, we found it hard to know where to start: there are no national exams, every school does things differently, and the tests seem to come round quickly. This is what's helped us so far, checked against the research on how people learn. We're still finding our way, and their teachers' advice always comes first.

The short version

  1. KS3 tests follow what's just been taught, so the revision list or the last few weeks of class notes is the place to start.
  2. 10 to 20 minutes on a school night in the week or two before a test is plenty.
  3. Test, don't re-read. Short quizzes and "what can you remember?" beat reading notes again.
  4. Spread it out: a little over several days works far better than one long session the night before.
  5. The habit matters more than the marks. Students who learn to revise now find GCSEs much less of a shock.

1. What KS3 tests are like

Key Stage 3 covers Years 7, 8 and 9 in England. There are no national exams at this stage, so each school sets its own tests. Usually that means:

  • End-of-topic tests after each unit, often every few weeks in maths, science and languages.
  • End-of-year (or mid-year) exams covering everything taught that year, sometimes sat in the hall to get students used to exam conditions.
  • Vocabulary and spelling tests in languages, often weekly.

Because tests follow what's just been taught, the best guide is the school's revision list. Many schools send one home or put it on their learning platform. If not, the last few weeks of exercise books and class notes tell you what's coming up.

2. Why revising at KS3 matters

KS3 results don't go on certificates, and we've come to see that as the point: it's a good time to practise. There's room to try things, get them wrong and work out what helps, without the pressure of GCSEs.

  • The basics build up. Much of GCSE maths, science and languages rests on what's taught in Years 7 to 9. Gaps left now come back later.
  • Revising is a skill. Most students aren't taught how to revise. Those who work it out by Year 9 start GCSEs with a real advantage.
  • Sets and options. Many schools use KS3 tests to help decide sets, and Year 9 results can feed into GCSE option choices.

3. How much is enough

Less than you might think. These are rough guides that have worked for us, and your child's school may suggest its own:

  • Before an end-of-topic test: 10 to 20 minutes on two or three evenings in the week before.
  • Before end-of-year exams: 20 to 30 minutes most school days for the two or three weeks before, one or two subjects at a time.
  • The rest of the time: a few minutes a day going back over older topics keeps them fresh, and makes the next test easier.

Short and regular beats long and occasional. A tired Year 7 doing an hour on a Sunday night won't remember much of it by Thursday.

4. Simple methods that tend to work

Younger students often think revising means reading their notes again, or making them look neat. Both feel productive, but the research suggests they don't help much. These tend to work better:

Quiz yourself

Questions, flashcards, or covering the page and saying what you remember. Pulling an answer out of memory is what makes it stick, and getting it wrong first is part of how it works.

The blank page

Write the topic at the top of a blank page, then everything you can remember about it in five minutes. Then check the notes and add what was missed, in a different colour. The gaps show exactly what to go over.

Little and often

Coming back to a topic over several days, rather than all in one go, is what makes it last. It's also easier to fit around clubs and homework.

Explain it to someone

Explaining a topic out loud, to you, a sibling or even the dog, shows up what they don't really understand yet.

Learn vocabulary in small batches

For languages, 10 words a day over a week works far better than 50 the night before. Saying them out loud and writing them from memory both help.

5. How you can help

A few things that have made a difference for us:

  • Help them find out what's coming up. Ask "what's your next test on?" and look at the revision list together.
  • Make a tiny plan. Which evenings, which subject, ten minutes each. Writing it down makes it far more likely to happen.
  • Be the quiz master, lightly. Reading out questions or flashcards is one of the easiest ways to help, and you don't need to know the subject yourself.
  • Praise the effort and the method, not just the mark. "You started early this time" is what builds the habit.
  • Keep it in proportion. A disappointing Year 8 test is useful information, not a disaster. Ask what they'd do differently next time.

Signs they might need more help

  • The same subject keeps going badly, test after test.
  • They can't explain what the test was on, or what they got wrong.
  • They're anxious about tests in a way that's affecting sleep or mood.

In any of these cases, a quick message to the subject teacher or form tutor is the best first step. Teachers would much rather know early.

6. Year 9 and getting ready for GCSEs

Year 9 is when many students choose their GCSE options (see our Year 9 options guide), and some schools start GCSE content in Year 9. It's a good year to:

  • Move from 10-minute sessions towards 20 or 30 minutes, a few times a week.
  • Get used to testing themselves rather than re-reading.
  • Practise longer written answers, which become much more important at GCSE.
  • Start each school year by finding out which exam board each GCSE subject will use.

Common questions

Does my child need to revise for KS3 tests?

A little, yes. The marks matter less than at GCSE, but short, regular revision before tests builds the habit and fills gaps before they grow. Ten to twenty minutes on a few evenings is usually enough.

Do KS3 results matter?

They don't go on any certificate, but many schools use them to set classes and to help with GCSE option choices in Year 9. More importantly, they show where the gaps are while there's plenty of time to fix them.

Are there SATs in Year 9?

No. National tests at age 14 (KS3 SATs) stopped in 2008. Schools now set their own tests and end-of-year exams.

My child says they have nothing to revise. What should I do?

Ask when the next test is, and look at their recent classwork together. Going back over the last few weeks of any subject is always useful, even without a test coming up.

Should my Year 7 use past papers?

Not usually. KS3 tests are set by each school, so past GCSE papers won't match. Questions made from their own notes and revision lists are a much better fit.

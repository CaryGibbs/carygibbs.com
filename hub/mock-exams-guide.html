Mock exams: what parents need to know

Mocks can feel like a big deal at home. Here's what we've learned about what they're for, how to help, and what to do with the results.

By Cary Gibbs, dad of two and founder of Lobe Limpet · Updated November 2026 · About a 5-minute read

A note before you start. I'm a dad, not a teacher. Mocks were the first time exams felt real in our house, and we weren't sure how much to worry about them. This is what we've found helps, checked against the research on how people learn. Schools run mocks differently, so their advice comes first.

The short version

  1. Mocks are practice, usually in Year 11 and Year 13, run like the real exams.
  2. The feedback matters more than the grade. They show exactly what to work on before the summer.
  3. Prepare the same way as for the real thing: little and often, testing themselves, past papers.
  4. Afterwards, go through the papers together and turn the dropped marks into a plan.
  5. Keep it in proportion. A disappointing mock is useful information, not a prediction set in stone.

1. What mocks are for

Mocks are practice exams, set by the school and usually using real past papers. They're run in exam conditions, often in the hall, with invigilators and timings like the real thing. They help in three ways:

  • Practice at exams themselves: timing, nerves, reading questions carefully, and getting through a full paper.
  • A clear picture of the gaps, while there's still time to fix them.
  • Information for the school: mocks often help decide predicted grades, tiers (foundation or higher), and sometimes sixth form or college references.

2. When they happen

Most schools hold Year 11 mocks somewhere between November and February, and some hold a second round in the spring. Year 13 mocks are often around January. Your child's school will publish its own dates, and it's worth putting them on the family calendar early.

3. In the weeks before

What's helped us is treating mocks as a rehearsal for the summer, not a separate event:

  • Find out what's being tested. Some mocks cover the whole course, others only what's been taught so far. Ask, or check the revision list.
  • Start a few weeks out, little and often. Twenty to forty minutes most days beats cramming in the last week.
  • Test, don't re-read. Quizzes, flashcards, past paper questions and the blank page method all work better than reading notes again.
  • Do at least one past paper under timed conditions per subject, if they can. It's the closest thing to the real experience.
  • Help them make a simple plan, with the shakiest subjects coming up most often and some rest days built in.

4. During mock week

  • Sleep and breakfast matter more than a late-night cram.
  • Check the kit: pens, a calculator with working batteries, a clear pencil case, a water bottle.
  • Keep evenings light: a short look over the next day's topics, then time off.
  • Don't do a post-mortem after every paper. "How did it go?" is fine; going through every question rarely helps the next exam.

5. After the results

This is where mocks earn their keep. What's worked for us:

  1. Start with what went well. There's always something.
  2. Look at the papers and the feedback together, if they're happy to. Which questions lost the most marks?
  3. Sort the dropped marks: didn't know it, knew it but couldn't explain it, ran out of time, or misread the question. Each needs a different fix.
  4. Turn it into a plan: the topics that lost marks become the first things to revise for the summer.
  5. Talk to the teacher if a result is a real surprise, or if your child is worried about which tier they'll sit.

Keeping it in proportion

Mock grades are a snapshot, often taken before the whole course has been taught or revised. Many students improve a lot between mocks and the summer. A low mock is the most useful kind: it shows exactly where to focus.

Common questions

Do mock results count towards final grades?

No. GCSE and A level grades come from the summer exams (and any coursework). Schools may use mocks to help decide predicted grades, tiers and references, so they're worth taking seriously.

How long should my child revise for mocks?

A few weeks of little and often works better than a big push at the end: around 20 to 40 minutes most days, building up nearer the time. Schools often suggest their own amounts.

My child did badly in their mocks. What now?

Go through the papers together, sort out why the marks were lost, and make those topics the start of the summer plan. If you're worried, ask the subject teacher what they'd focus on.

Should they use past papers?

Yes. Past papers and mark schemes are free on the exam boards' websites and are the best way to practise for the real thing.

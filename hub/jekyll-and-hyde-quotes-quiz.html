Jekyll and Hyde quotes quiz (GCSE English Literature)

Twelve questions on the quotes and moments that come up most in Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde. Then the key quotes, themes and context to go back over.

For GCSE English Literature: AQA, Edexcel, OCR and Eduqas · Updated November 2026

Jekyll and Hyde in a nutshell

  1. The story: the lawyer Utterson investigates the link between his respected friend Dr Jekyll and the violent Mr Hyde, and discovers they are the same man.
  2. Big themes: duality (good and evil), reputation, science and religion, violence, and secrecy.
  3. Context: written by Robert Louis Stevenson in 1886, when Victorian gentlemen were expected to be outwardly respectable.

The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Narrative

1. Whose point of view do we follow for most of the novella?

Most of the story follows Utterson, a lawyer, as he investigates. The truth only comes out at the end, in Lanyon's and Jekyll's written accounts, which keeps the mystery going.

Chapter 1

2. In Enfield's story, what does Hyde do to a young girl?

Hyde tramples calmly over a girl in the street. Enfield compares him to "some damned Juggernaut". Hyde then pays the family with a cheque signed by Jekyll.

Chapter 1

3. Enfield says there is "something displeasing, something downright detestable" about Hyde. What is he describing?

People feel disgust at Hyde's appearance, but can't say exactly what is wrong with it. This vagueness makes him more frightening and mysterious.

Chapter 2

4. Who says "If he be Mr. Hyde… I shall be Mr. Seek"?

Utterson's pun shows his determination to find Hyde and uncover the secret. It also turns the story into a kind of detective mystery.

Chapter 2

5. Utterson says he has read "Satan's signature upon a face". What does this suggest about Hyde?

After meeting Hyde, Utterson links him to the devil. Hyde is repeatedly described as evil, deformed and less than human.

Chapter 4

6. Who does Hyde murder?

A maid sees Hyde beat Sir Danvers Carew, an elderly MP, to death with a cane. The broken cane is later traced back to Jekyll.

Chapter 4

7. The maid says Hyde attacked with "ape-like fury". What does this suggest?

Comparing Hyde to an ape suggests he is less evolved, more animal than human. Victorian readers would link this to Darwin's theory of evolution and fears of humans going backwards.

Chapter 6

8. What happens to Dr Lanyon?

Lanyon tells Utterson he has had a shock he will never recover from, and soon dies. His letter, opened later, reveals he saw Hyde transform into Jekyll.

Chapter 10

9. True or false: Jekyll first takes the potion to separate the good and evil sides of himself.

True. In his full statement, Jekyll explains that he wanted to separate his good and bad sides, so he could enjoy his secret pleasures without damaging his reputation.

Chapter 10

10. Jekyll writes that "man is not truly one, but truly two". What idea does this express?

This is the central idea of duality: every person has a good and an evil side. Jekyll's mistake is trying to separate them.

Setting

11. How is London often described in the novella?

Thick fog and dark, empty streets create a mysterious, threatening atmosphere. The fog also reflects the secrets being hidden.

Chapter 10

12. Why can't Jekyll stop turning into Hyde in the end?

Jekyll starts changing into Hyde without the potion, and needs larger doses to change back. When his supply runs out, new salt doesn't work: the original batch must have been impure.

Key quotes to learn

  • "lean, long, dusty, dreary and yet somehow lovable" (narrator on Utterson, Chapter 1): respectable, but warm.
  • "like some damned Juggernaut" (Enfield, Chapter 1): Hyde's unstoppable violence.
  • "something displeasing, something downright detestable" (Enfield, Chapter 1): Hyde's appearance.
  • "If he be Mr. Hyde… I shall be Mr. Seek" (Utterson, Chapter 2): the investigation.
  • "Satan's signature upon a face" (Utterson, Chapter 2): Hyde as evil.
  • "ape-like fury" (the maid's account, Chapter 4): Hyde as primitive.
  • "I have had a shock… and I shall never recover" (Lanyon, Chapter 6): the horror of the truth.
  • "man is not truly one, but truly two" (Jekyll, Chapter 10): duality.

Themes: key points

  • Duality: everyone has a good and evil side. Jekyll tries to separate them and is destroyed.
  • Reputation: Victorian gentlemen protect their reputations, so secrets are kept and questions aren't asked.
  • Science and religion: Jekyll's experiments go against nature and God. Lanyon represents more traditional science.
  • Violence: Hyde's attacks on the girl and on Carew show what happens when evil is let loose.
  • Secrecy and silence: letters, locked doors and things left unsaid build the mystery.

Context: key points

  • Published in 1886 by Robert Louis Stevenson, a Scottish writer.
  • Victorian gentlemen were expected to be outwardly respectable, which led many to hide their real behaviour.
  • Darwin's theory of evolution (1859) raised fears that humans might have a primitive, animal side.
  • Rapid advances in science worried people about scientists going too far.
  • London had rich, respectable areas next to poor, crime-ridden streets, mirroring the novella's split between good and evil.

Revising Jekyll and Hyde

The exam is closed book, so learning short quotes is the key. A few things that tend to help:

  • Learn short quotes for each main character: Jekyll, Hyde, Utterson and Lanyon.
  • Link each quote to a theme, especially duality and reputation.
  • Quiz yourself little and often: say the start of the quote and finish it, or say who says it and when.
  • Remember the structure: the truth is held back until the last two chapters, and that's worth writing about.

Common questions

Is the GCSE English Literature exam open book?

No. Since 2017, GCSE English Literature exams are closed book for all the main exam boards, so you need to learn quotes from memory.

What is the full title of the novella?

Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde. It was published in 1886 and has ten chapters.

How many quotes should I learn for Jekyll and Hyde?

Around 10 to 15 short quotes, covering the main characters and themes, is a good target.

What does the exam question look like?

For most boards you're given an extract and asked about a character or theme, in the extract and in the novella as a whole. Check the exact format with your teacher.

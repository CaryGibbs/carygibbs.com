The quiz

Have a go before you look at the key points. Getting some wrong is fine: trying to remember is what makes it stick, and the answers show you what to go back over.

Biology

1. Which part of a cell controls what the cell does?

The nucleus contains the genetic material and controls the cell's activities. The cell membrane controls what goes in and out; the cell wall (plants only) gives support.

Biology

2. How do plants make their own food?

Plants make glucose by photosynthesis, using light energy, carbon dioxide and water. They take water and minerals from the soil, but not their food.

Biology

3. Where in the body does gas exchange happen?

Oxygen passes into the blood, and carbon dioxide passes out, through the tiny air sacs in the lungs called alveoli. They have thin walls and a large surface area.

Chemistry

4. In which state of matter are the particles close together but able to move past each other?

In a liquid, particles touch but can move past each other, so liquids flow. In a solid they vibrate in fixed positions; in a gas they are far apart and move quickly in all directions.

Chemistry

5. What is a compound?

A compound contains two or more elements chemically joined, such as water (H2O). An element has only one type of atom. In a mixture, substances aren't chemically joined.

Chemistry

6. What colour does universal indicator turn in a strong acid?

Strong acids turn universal indicator red (pH 1 to 2). Neutral solutions (pH 7) turn it green, and strong alkalis turn it purple.

Physics

7. True or false: mass and weight are the same thing.

False. Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. Weight is the force of gravity on it, measured in newtons. On the Moon your mass stays the same but your weight is less.

Physics

8. What is the unit of force?

Force is measured in newtons (N), using a newton meter. Energy is measured in joules and power in watts.

Physics

9. A cyclist travels 100 m in 20 s. What is their average speed?

Speed = distance ÷ time = 100 ÷ 20 = 5 m/s.

Physics

10. In a series circuit with two bulbs, what happens if one bulb breaks?

A series circuit has only one path, so a break anywhere stops the current and both bulbs go out. In a parallel circuit, the other bulb would stay on.

Physics

11. True or false: light travels faster than sound.

True. Light travels at about 300,000 km per second; sound in air at about 340 m per second. That's why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

Physics

12. What causes the seasons on Earth?

The Earth's axis is tilted. When our half of the Earth is tilted towards the Sun, we get summer: longer days and more direct sunlight. Day and night are caused by the Earth spinning.

Biology: key points

  • Cells: animal cells have a nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane and mitochondria. Plant cells also have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a vacuole.
  • Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organisms.
  • Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light. Respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, releasing energy.
  • The body: the heart pumps blood; the lungs exchange gases in the alveoli; the digestive system breaks food down so it can be absorbed.
  • A balanced diet includes carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water.
  • Variation can be inherited, environmental or both. Food chains and webs show how energy passes between organisms.

Chemistry: key points

  • States of matter: solid, liquid and gas, explained by how the particles are arranged and move.
  • Elements contain one type of atom; compounds have elements chemically joined; mixtures aren't chemically joined and can be separated.
  • Separating mixtures: filtering, evaporation, distillation and chromatography.
  • Acids and alkalis: the pH scale runs from 1 (strong acid) through 7 (neutral) to 14 (strong alkali). Acid + alkali → salt + water (neutralisation).
  • In a chemical reaction, new substances are made. Signs include a colour change, fizzing, or a temperature change.
  • The periodic table lists all the elements: metals on the left, non-metals on the right.

Physics: key points

  • Forces are pushes or pulls, measured in newtons. Balanced forces mean no change in motion.
  • Weight is the force of gravity on a mass. Speed = distance ÷ time.
  • Energy is stored and transferred, never created or destroyed. It's measured in joules.
  • Electricity: series circuits have one path; parallel circuits have more than one. Current is measured in amps, potential difference in volts.
  • Light travels in straight lines and reflects and refracts. Sound needs a medium to travel through.
  • Space: day and night come from the Earth spinning; the seasons come from the tilt of its axis; a year is one orbit of the Sun.

Revising for KS3 Science tests

KS3 tests are set by each school, so start with the revision list or the last few weeks of class notes. A few things that tend to help:

  • Quiz first, then check the notes. Trying to remember is what makes it stick.
  • 10 to 20 minutes on a few evenings before a test is plenty for most students.
  • Learn the key words, such as nucleus, compound and newton. Science tests reward the right vocabulary.
  • Practise the simple calculations, such as speed, until they're automatic.

Common questions

What does KS3 Science cover?

Biology, chemistry and physics, usually taught in Years 7, 8 and 9 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (S1 to S3 in Scotland). Each school chooses its own order and tests.

Are there national tests in KS3 Science?

No. KS3 SATs stopped in 2008. Schools set their own end-of-topic tests and end-of-year exams.

How should my child revise for a KS3 Science test?

Start with the school's revision list, then use short quizzes and flashcards over a few evenings, rather than re-reading notes the night before.

Is KS3 Science important for GCSE?

Yes. Much of GCSE Science builds on KS3 topics such as cells, particles, forces and energy, so gaps left now often show up later.
